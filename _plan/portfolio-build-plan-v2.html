01

What changed from v1 and why it matters.

What the resume changed

Three things in your resume moved the strategy, and one is a genuine advantage you may not be counting.

1. Clients became employers

The original brief said “turn visitors into clients.” You're after a full-time role, so the conversion event is an interview request, not a project inquiry. Practically: the primary action becomes “View my resume” or “Let's talk,” not “Book a call.” No pricing, no engagement models, no availability-for-hire framing — those actively confuse a recruiter. This also explains why resume tracking matters to you and wouldn't to a consultant.

2. You're a developer, not a designer

This is good news. A recruiter can't assess React skill from a paragraph claiming React skill — but they can assess it from a site that loads fast, works flawlessly on their phone, handles keyboard navigation, and scores well when they open Lighthouse. For a design consultant, polish is decoration. For you, polish is evidence. Your ambition for an award-level site is therefore fully aligned with getting hired, which is unusual and worth exploiting.

3. You have something rare, even though you didn't lead with it

You chose the broader positioning, which is the right call for funnel width at two years in. But keep this in view: drone thermal imagery and solar inspection automation is work almost no React/Node developer has touched. It shouldn't be your headline — it would narrow you — but it should absolutely be your flagship case study. It's the thing an interviewer will remember and ask about, and it's the reason your generalist claim becomes believable rather than generic.

The synthesis

Lead with a broad, legible claim so you match a wide set of job descriptions. Prove it with a specific, memorable project nobody else has. Width in the headline, depth in the evidence — you don't have to choose.

02

Both answers you picked, resolved into one claim.

Positioning & hero copy

You picked full-stack React + AWS and frontend specialist with backend depth. Those aren't in conflict — combined, they're sharper than either alone, because “frontend developer who genuinely ships the backend too” is a real and in-demand profile, while “full-stack generalist” is a crowd.

I build React interfaces — and the AWS services behind them. Proof line: Two years shipping production features at Hornbill Technologies: a React + Node portal, serverless pipelines on Lambda and DynamoDB, and Python automation for drone-based solar inspection reporting.

Two alternates, if that one feels off

  • Warmer: “Frontend-focused full-stack developer. I turn Figma files into fast, responsive React — and I own the Lambda functions underneath.”
  • Outcome-led: “I ship the whole feature. React and TypeScript on the front, Node and AWS serverless behind it — from Figma handoff to CloudWatch dashboard.”

All three do the same three jobs: name the stack a recruiter is keyword-matching, stake a clear frontend lean, and signal backend credibility in the same breath. Pick one and use it verbatim on the site, your LinkedIn headline, and the resume summary — consistency across all three is itself a signal.

On the “Senior” title

Senior Developer at roughly two years post-graduation is unusual, and some reviewers will discount it while others won't notice. Keep it — it's your real title — but make the site back it with scope: what you owned end to end, what you decided, what you were trusted with alone. Evidence of ownership is what makes the title read as earned rather than inflated.

Turn the EEE degree into an asset

Electrical & Electronics Engineering to full-stack development is not a gap to explain away — it's a story that differentiates you, and it's directly relevant given you now write software for solar panel thermal inspection. The domain knowledge is real. One or two lines on the About page: you understand the physical systems your software measures. Most developers on that team don't.

Resume: what to fix first

The site will reuse this content, so the problems propagate. These are worth an hour before any code gets written.

The career objective is working against you

Current text:

“Full-stack developer with experience in React.js, Node.js, AWS, and responsive UI engineering. Strong problem-solving mindset with the ability to learn fast and adapt to different roles. Excited to work across development, testing, and support functions while growing as a well-rounded software engineer.”

The first sentence is fine. The rest is quietly costing you interviews:

  • “ability to learn fast and adapt” is what candidates write when they have nothing specific to point at. You have specific things to point at.
  • “development, testing, and support functions” reads as I'll take anything. It contradicts a Senior title and invites lower-scoped offers.
  • “growing as a well-rounded software engineer” describes what you want. A summary should describe what the employer gets.

Replace with the claim plus proof from section 02. Roughly: “Full-stack developer specialising in React and TypeScript interfaces backed by AWS serverless. Two years at Hornbill Technologies building production features across a React + Node portal, Lambda/DynamoDB pipelines, and Python automation for drone-based solar inspection.” Same length, entirely different signal.

The blocking gap: you have no numbers

Your resume says “improving system performance,” “faster load times,” and “improving processing speed significantly” — three claims with no magnitude. Unquantified impact reads as unverified impact, and this is the single highest-leverage fix available to you. Go find these numbers this week:

  • Report automation: how long did a solar inspection report take manually vs. now? Hours to minutes? How many reports per month?
  • Load time: before/after on the portal pages you optimised. Even a rough Lighthouse or network-tab figure.
  • Scale: how many drone images, thermal scans, or inspection records does the pipeline process? Per run, per day?
  • Ownership: how many features did you ship? Was any of it yours alone, end to end?
  • Team: team size, your role in it, whether you reviewed others' code or mentored anyone.

If exact figures are unavailable, a defensible approximation you can explain in an interview beats silence. “Cut report preparation from about four hours to under ten minutes” is worth more than every adjective on the page.

Three smaller fixes

  • Drop Hobbies, Languages, and 10th-grade results. At two years' experience with a real senior title, school marks pull the reader's attention toward the least relevant thing about you. Keep the B.Tech and CGPA; cut the rest to make room for the numbers above.
  • Cut “Soft Skills.” A list of five self-assessed traits persuades nobody. Demonstrate them in how the case studies are written instead.
  • Clean up the repo. Your current portfolio still ships public/Resume 2025.pdf. A stale filename on a live site is exactly the detail a careful reviewer notices.

Site structure

Fewer pages than a consultant's site needs. A recruiter's patience is the binding constraint, and every extra page is somewhere they can fail to find the resume.

Notes on choices:

  • No /work index. Three case studies live on the homepage. One less click between a hiring manager and your best work.
  • No separate /contact. An anchored section on the homepage plus a persistent nav button. A contact page for a job search is a page nobody needs to load.
  • An HTML resume at /resume is the important one. It's the difference between knowing “someone opened a PDF” and knowing “this person spent ninety seconds and read the AWS section twice.” Section 08 covers this.
  • Nav: Work · About · Resume, plus a contrasting Get in touch. Four items, visible on every page, no hamburger on desktop.

The three case studies

You have one employer, which is a real constraint on portfolio variety. The fix is to make one of the three a project entirely your own — and conveniently, this site's resume tracker is exactly that.

01
Flagship — solar inspection report automation

Angle: a manual, slow, error-prone reporting process turned into an automated pipeline. Python, AWS, drone thermal data.

Why it's the flagship: it has a clear before/after, it's genuinely unusual, and it demonstrates the thing employers most want to see — that you find expensive manual work and remove it. Lead the page with the time saved.

Interactive element that earns its place: a stripped-down visual of the pipeline — thermal image in, defect detection, report out — that advances as the reader scrolls. Diagram, not decoration.

02
Depth — the React + Node portal

Angle: your frontend claim, evidenced. Figma handoff to component library, responsive across four device classes, load-time optimisation, API integration.

Make it concrete: pick one component or one screen that was genuinely hard and go deep on it. One well-explained hard problem outperforms a tour of ten easy ones. A before/after on load time belongs in the headline.

03
Fully yours — this site and its resume tracker

Angle: Next.js, API routes, DynamoDB, tokenised links, a small dashboard. Written up as a real engineering decision log: what you chose, what you rejected, what the privacy considerations were.

Why this is smarter than it looks: it's live, the reviewer is already inside it, there's no NDA to navigate, and it proves the exact AWS/serverless stack on your resume. It also quietly demonstrates that you think about ethics and edge cases — which is a senior signal.

Structure for each one

Lead with the outcome, then earn it backwards. Not chronology — nobody is convinced by your discovery phase.

  1. Outcome — the number, largest type on the page, one sentence of what changed.
  2. Context and constraint — what was broken, what was at stake, what you couldn't change.
  3. Two or three real decisions — what you believed, what you found, what you did differently. This is where an interviewer decides whether to talk to you.
  4. Stack and why — not a logo wall. Why DynamoDB and not Postgres. Why Lambda and not a container. Short, specific, defensible.
  5. What you'd change — two sentences of honest hindsight. Rare, and it reads as seniority.
  6. Next case study + contact. Never a dead end.
Check this before publishing

Your Hornbill work may be commercially sensitive — client names, inspection data, defect rates, internal architecture. Write the case studies at the level of problem class and approach, keep real client identities and data out, and if you're unsure, ask your manager before it goes live. “A solar asset operator” carries the same weight as a name, with none of the risk. Publishing something you shouldn't have is a much bigger problem than a slightly vaguer case study.

You asked for the options first. Start with what's actually knowable.

Resume tracking, honestly

Before the options: the core constraint. You can only know who opened your resume if you gave that specific person a link nobody else has. Identity comes from the link you issued, never from the file itself. Everything else is anonymous traffic data.

So “track who opened my resume” splits into two different questions with different answers: did the person I applied to at Company X look at this? (solvable, and useful) and who are the strangers viewing my resume from my site? (not solvable without asking them).

The four approaches

My recommendation: A + C. A for outbound applications, where you know which token went to which company and the information genuinely changes what you do. C for everything else. Skip B — the friction costs more than the names are worth. D only if you'd rather not build it.

The much better version: an HTML resume, not a PDF

If /resume is a real page rather than a PDF, tracking stops being binary. A PDF open tells you one bit of information. A page view tells you how long they stayed, how far they scrolled, which sections they lingered on, and whether they came back three days later — which is the single strongest buying signal you can observe.

Keep the PDF as a download for ATS uploads and for people who want to print it. But make the link you send a page.

How it works

// app/api/r/[token]/route.ts
export async function GET(req, { params }) {
  const { token } = params

  // 1. resolve the token you issued
  const rec = await db.get(token)          // { company, role, contact }
  if (!rec) return redirect('/resume')     // unknown token: serve anyway

  // 2. record the event
  await db.putEvent({
    token,
    ts:       Date.now(),
    ua:       req.headers.get('user-agent'),
    referrer: req.headers.get('referer'),
    country:  req.geo?.country,            // coarse, from the edge
    city:     req.geo?.city,
  })

  // 3. serve the resume, personalised if you like
  return redirect(`/resume?ref=${token}`)
}

Then /resume posts lightweight scroll-depth and dwell events to /api/track, and /admin lists it back to you: Company X · Senior React role · opened twice · 2m14s · reached the AWS section. Persist to DynamoDB, which you already know, and issue tokens from a small private page so generating one takes five seconds while you're applying.

What this will get wrong

Know these before you start reading meaning into the data:

  • Email clients cause false opens. Apple Mail Privacy Protection, Gmail's proxy, and corporate security scanners pre-fetch links to check them. A hit seconds after you send is probably a machine. Trust dwell time and scroll depth, not the open count.
  • Location is approximate and often wrong. City-level at best; VPNs and corporate networks will report a different country entirely.
  • Forwarding is invisible — mostly. You can't see that your link was passed to three colleagues. But one token opening from several distinct devices or cities is a strong hint it's circulating internally, which is good news.
  • Most large-company applications will never appear. If you upload a PDF into an ATS, your link is never involved. Tokenised links only work where you email or message a human directly.
  • An open is not interest. Someone may open, glance, and close. Don't let a notification change your behaviour more than the information warrants.

Doing this cleanly

This is legitimate — it's the same category of thing every newsletter and sales tool does — but the version that stays legitimate has two properties:

  • Disclose it. One line on the resume page: “This page records views so I know when to follow up.” Costs you nothing, and it means you're never in the position of a recruiter discovering it and drawing conclusions about your judgment.
  • Don't do the covert versions. No tracking pixels embedded in PDFs or emails. They're technically unreliable — most viewers block remote content, so you get silence rather than data — and they read as sneaky if noticed. The tokenised link is both more accurate and defensible.

Worth knowing: IP addresses and behavioural data are personal data under India's DPDP Act and under GDPR if a European recruiter views your page. For a personal site at this scale nobody is coming after you, but a short privacy note at /privacy is twenty minutes of work and makes the whole thing unambiguous. It also happens to be a detail a thoughtful interviewer will notice approvingly.

What it's genuinely for

Not surveillance — timing. Knowing that someone at a company you applied to read your resume twice yesterday tells you exactly when a follow-up message is welcome rather than annoying. That's the real return, and it's substantial.

For you, this section is the portfolio — not the packaging.

Craft specification

Everything here is assessable by the people deciding whether to interview you. Treat each item as a question you'll be scored on.

Performance

  • Lighthouse 95+ on mobile. Assume an engineer will run it. Next.js gives you most of this if you don't fight it — use next/image, next/font, and static generation for the case studies.
  • LCP under 2.5s on a throttled 4G profile. Portfolio sites die on hero imagery; serve modern formats at correct sizes.
  • No layout shift. A CLS jump on your own site is a visible failure of the exact skill you're claiming.

Accessibility

  • Semantic HTML, one h1, real landmarks. Keyboard-navigable throughout with visible focus states.
  • Contrast at AA minimum, both themes. Alt text on every image that carries meaning.
  • For a frontend role this is directly assessed, and most candidate portfolios fail it — which makes it cheap differentiation.

Responsive

  • Test at 360px, 390px, 768px, 1280px, 1920px. Your resume claims responsive engineering across device classes; the site is where that claim is checked.
  • Case studies on mobile are a reading experience: single column, full-bleed images, generous type. Not a shrunken desktop layout.
  • Every hover behaviour needs a touch equivalent. Hover-only content doesn't exist for half your visitors.

Motion

One rule: motion clarifies state or structure; it never gates content.

ElementSpec
Page loadNo preloader. The hero text is present at first paint, always.
Scroll revealsFire at 15–20% viewport entry, 300–400ms, ease-out. Subtle enough that it reads as quality, not as effect.
Hover statesUnder 150ms. They confirm interactivity; that's all.
Route transitionsOptional. If used, 400–600ms with visual continuity. Never block navigation.
Reduced motionprefers-reduced-motion honoured throughout, losing no information.

Restraint is the flex here. Sparse, precise motion reads as a developer who understands performance budgets. Heavy motion reads as someone who discovered a library.

Repository hygiene

  • README with a screenshot, live link, stack list, and local setup steps. This is the first file the engineer opens.
  • Meaningful commit messages. A history of “update” × 40 is a signal, and not a good one.
  • .gitignore correct, no secrets, no stale files, no Resume 2025.pdf.
  • Sensible component structure with clear names. Rename src/My Components — a space in a directory name is the kind of thing reviewers quietly notice.

Build order
